# Card 3: Property tax direction for residential ratepayers

### TOPIC
Should Toronto residential property taxes be held to inflation or below (Bradford), or remain a primary lever for fiscal sustainability with at-or-above-inflation increases plus progressive add-ons (Chow)?

### SLUG
property-tax-direction-residential

### PULL QUOTE
Toronto's residential rate ran at or below inflation for most of 2010 to 2022, then rose 7.0% in 2023, 9.5% in 2024, and 6.9% in 2025 before the election-year 2026 budget settled at 2.2%. The city's own ten-year capital plan grew to $63.1 billion in 2026, with an $18 billion state-of-good-repair gap still outstanding, so the question is less whether residential taxpayers fund the gap and more on what schedule.

### WHO BENEFITS

#### Bradford's mechanism
Restraint at or below inflation reduces the year-over-year cash burden on owner-occupiers and pass-through to tenants in the short run. Property tax incidence research is split: from the perspective of a single jurisdiction the local tax is borne mainly by local residents and is roughly proportional to housing consumption (Mieszkowski and Zodrow, 1989), and assessment-based regressivity has been documented at the national level in U.S. data (Avenancio-Leon and Howard, 2022). However, Toronto's own infrastructure backlog means restraint shifts cost into deferred capital, debt service, or service reductions whose incidence falls on transit riders, social housing tenants, and users of city facilities (City of Toronto, 2025).

#### Chow's mechanism
At-or-above-inflation residential increases plus progressive add-ons (3% Vacant Home Tax, graduated luxury MLTT to 8.6%) raise the immediate cash burden on owner-occupiers while drawing additional revenue from non-occupied units and high-value transactions. The "new view" of property tax incidence holds that the nationwide-average burden falls on capital owners and is progressive, while the above-average local component drives capital out of high-tax jurisdictions and is borne by local residents (Mieszkowski, 1972; Zodrow, 2001). Tassonyi, Bird, and Slack (2015) found GTA municipalities had not exhausted the revenue-raising side of the property tax curve, suggesting room to raise rates without crossing the revenue-hill peak, though they flagged tax-competition effects between Toronto and surrounding municipalities.

#### Literature
Mieszkowski on the new view (T3, 1972) . Mieszkowski and Zodrow on incidence views (T3, 1989) . Zodrow on the property tax as capital tax (T3, 2001) . Tassonyi, Bird, and Slack on GTA tax competition and revenue hills (T2, 2015) . Slack and Bird on property tax reform (T2, 2015) . Avenancio-Leon and Howard on assessment regressivity (T3, 2022) . Caracciolo and Miglino on Vancouver Empty Homes Tax (T2, 2024) . IMFG / Slack on Canadian municipal fiscal sustainability (T2, 2024).

### POSITIONS

#### Brad Bradford
In his 2023 mayoral byelection platform Bradford committed to "keep property tax increases at or below the rate of inflation" (Global News promise tracker, 2023). In council, he voted against the 2025 6.9% increase, called it "a record-breaking tax increase," and on the 2026 budget pressed Mayor Chow on whether she would commit to keeping property taxes at the rate of inflation in a future term, characterizing the 2026 reduction as a pre-election move financed from reserves (CBC News, March 5, 2025; CBC News, February 11, 2026).

#### Olivia Chow
Chow's three budgets delivered a 9.5% residential increase in 2024, 6.9% in 2025, and 2.2% in 2026 (City of Toronto, 2024, 2025, 2026), with the lower 2026 figure attributed to revenue from progressive levies (3% Vacant Home Tax, graduated luxury MLTT) rather than a permanent shift in approach (CBC News, February 4, 2026). On the 2024 budget Chow noted that closing the $1.776 billion opening pressure with residential property taxes alone would have required a 42% increase (City of Toronto, 2024).

### STATUS QUO

What is actually happening in Toronto now (2026 budget, adopted February 10, 2026):
- Operating budget: $18.9 billion ($16.61 billion tax-supported, $2.25 billion rate-supported) (City of Toronto, 2026).
- Ten-year capital plan: $63.1 billion (2026 to 2035) (Ontario Construction News, 2026).
- 2026 residential property tax increase: 0.7% base plus 1.5% City Building Fund equals 2.2% combined (CBC News, January 7, 2026).
- Approximate annual impact on the typical home: about $91.53 per year (City of Toronto news release, 2026).
- State-of-good-repair gap: $18 billion over the next decade as of 2025, down from a previously reported $26 billion (CBC News, May 2025).

#### Existing policy stack
- City Building Fund levy at 1.5% per year, dedicated to transit and housing capital, in place through 2025 and continued in 2026 (City of Toronto Council, December 2019).
- Vacant Home Tax raised from 1% to 3% for the 2024 tax year, with projected annual revenue of approximately $105 million (City of Toronto, 2024).
- Municipal Land Transfer Tax with graduated luxury rates: 4.4% for $3 to $4 million, scaling up to 8.6% for $20 million-plus, effective from 2024 increase, projected $152 million in 2026 (City of Toronto Council, December 2025; CBC News, December 17, 2025).
- 15% reduction in property tax rates for more than 28,000 small business properties continued in 2025 (City of Toronto, 2025).
- Multi-residential, commercial, and industrial property classes taxed at separate rates from residential (City of Toronto, 2025).

### COMPARABLE JURISDICTIONS

**1. Toronto, 2010 to 2026 (within-Toronto comparable).**
Year-by-year total residential increases (base plus dedicated levies) were 4.0% (2010), 0.0% (2011), 2.5% (2012), 2.0% (2013), 2.2% (2014), 2.75% (2015), 1.9% (2016), 2.5% (2017), 2.6% (2018), 3.05% (2019), 3.5% (2020), 2.2% (2021), 4.4% (2022), 7.0% (2023), 9.5% (2024), 6.9% (2025), 2.2% (2026) (Toronto Taxpayer compilation, 2026; consistent with City of Toronto annual rate by-laws).
Outcome: under restraint years (Ford-era 2011 to 2014 and the early Tory era through 2019), Toronto's state-of-good-repair backlog grew, and IMFG analysis observed that property tax increases at or below inflation in Canadian cities coincided with deteriorating municipal infrastructure (Slack, 2024, T2). Under the 2024 to 2025 increases, the city reported $8 billion of progress against the renewal gap and a $63.1 billion ten-year capital plan in 2026 (CBC News, 2025; Ontario Construction News, 2026).
Caveats: tax history mixes base rate, Scarborough Subway levy, and City Building Fund levy. Pandemic-era operating pressures (refugee shelter costs, transit ridership shortfall) are partial explanations for 2023 to 2025 increases and complicate clean attribution to mayoral preference (City of Toronto, 2024).

**2. Calgary, 2024 to 2026.**
Calgary council approved a plan in November 2023 to shift one percentage point of total tax share from non-residential to residential properties each year from 2024 through 2026, with a 2024 residential increase of 7.8% paired with 3.5% non-residential (CTV Calgary, 2023). Council approved a 3.6% overall increase for 2025 (CBC Calgary, 2024). In November 2025, council cancelled the planned 1% shift for 2026 and reduced the overall 2026 increase to 1.64%, replacing it with a smaller 0.25% annual shift starting in 2027 (City of Calgary, 2025; CTV Calgary, 2025).
Outcome: residential tax share moved from roughly 52:48 toward 55:45 then partially reversed. The 2026 reduction was achieved through reserve reallocation rather than a permanent revenue source (Sprawl Calgary, 2025).
Caveats: Alberta's provincial education property tax adds a separate top-line increase that compounds municipal decisions. Calgary's non-residential base differs in scale from Toronto's and faces sustained downtown office vacancy.

**3. Vancouver Empty Homes Tax, 2017 to 2025 (a non-residential-rate revenue tool).**
Vancouver's Empty Homes Tax has generated $194.3 million for affordable housing since 2017 and the number of declared or deemed-vacant homes fell to 979 in 2024, a 0.49% vacancy rate (City of Vancouver, 2025). Caracciolo and Miglino (2024, T2) used a difference-in-differences design across the Vancouver and Burnaby boundary and estimated the tax lowered vacancy by 1.5 percentage points and reduced empty units by approximately 5,355 between 2016 and 2021, with no measured effect on average rent.
Outcome: shifts a small share of revenue toward affordable housing capital and reduces measured vacancy, with limited near-term effect on rents.
Caveats: Vancouver and Toronto differ in housing market structure. Toronto's revamped 3% Vacant Home Tax projected approximately $105 million in 2024 revenue but has had administrative issues including the 2023 declaration program errors that prompted a 2024 program redesign (CBC News, 2024).

**4. Property tax incidence literature (cross-jurisdictional anchor, not a single jurisdiction).**
The "benefit view" treats property tax as a payment for local services and so as roughly non-distortionary; the "new view" pioneered by Mieszkowski (1972) treats nationwide-average property tax as a tax on capital owners (progressive) and the above-average local component as borne by local residents through capital outflows (Mieszkowski and Zodrow, 1989, T3; Zodrow, 2001, T3). Recent U.S. work documents systematic regressivity in assessment-based incidence at the parcel level (Avenancio-Leon and Howard, 2022, T3, Quarterly Journal of Economics). Tassonyi, Bird, and Slack (2015, T2) found GTA municipalities including Toronto had not reached the revenue-maximizing point on the property tax curve, suggesting available revenue room.
Caveats: the U.S. assessment-regressivity literature uses parcel-level sales-ratio data not directly transferable to MPAC's mass-appraisal regime in Ontario. Theoretical incidence views differ in assumed capital mobility and time horizon and are not directly comparable to a single-year council decision.

### PROJECTIONS

Literature does not support a confident singular projection on this question. The comparable-jurisdiction outcomes above are the closest defensible numerical anchors. Three plural framings that the literature does support:

1. Under sustained restraint (Bradford-style at or below inflation), the IMFG body of work predicts continuing growth in the state-of-good-repair backlog absent a substitute revenue source, consistent with the deferred-capital pattern observed in Toronto from roughly 2011 through 2022 (Slack, 2024, T2).
2. Under continued at-or-above-inflation residential increases (Chow trajectory), Tassonyi, Bird, and Slack (2015) suggest revenue-side room exists in the GTA but flag tax-competition effects on the non-residential base; the within-Toronto 2023 to 2025 history shows operating-budget stabilization paired with a $4-figure annual cost increase for the typical owner relative to a freeze counterfactual (City of Toronto budget summaries, 2024 to 2026).
3. Under a hybrid path (modest residential increases plus expanded progressive levies as in 2026), the documented revenue contributions are: Vacant Home Tax projected $105 million in 2024, luxury MLTT projected $152 million in 2026 (City of Toronto, 2024 to 2026); these are smaller than the operating-budget gap absorbed by the 2024 9.5% increase, so the literature does not support a claim that progressive levies alone substitute for residential rate decisions over a four-year term.

### TIME HORIZON

Property tax rate decisions affect household cash flow in year one and accumulate compoundingly across a four-year term. State-of-good-repair effects of restraint or expansion materialize across roughly five to fifteen years as deferred renewal feeds into asset failures and service disruptions (City of Toronto Corporate Asset Management Plan, 2025). Capital tax incidence effects under the new view are long-run general-equilibrium effects measured across decades (Mieszkowski and Zodrow, 1989).
