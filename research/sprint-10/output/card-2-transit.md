### TOPIC
How Toronto pays for the operating cost of running the TTC over 2026 to 2034, and what each candidate would do as the Ontario "New Deal" expires in 2027.

### SLUG
transit-operating-funding

### PULL QUOTE
Toronto's 2026 TTC operating budget passed at roughly $3.0 billion, with a $1.48 billion city subsidy and a $35 million reserve draw to hold fares flat (CBC, December 2025). The provincial "New Deal" funding that helps close the gap expires in 2027, and TTC documents project a $379 million shortfall in 2027 and a further $145 million shortfall in 2028 if it is not renewed (TorontoToday, December 2025).

### WHO BENEFITS

#### Bradford's mechanism
Bradford's stated approach combines fare-revenue protection (fare-evasion enforcement, service reliability), fiscal restraint at the property-tax line, and reliance on federal and provincial transfers rather than new municipal revenue tools. Incidence research on transit subsidies in Canada finds that transit operating support is broadly progressive at the rider level because lower-income households are over-represented among transit-dependent riders, but the distributional effect of how that subsidy is financed depends on whether the funding source is property tax, fare revenue, or sales-style instruments (Boisjoly et al., 2018). A reliance on existing transfers and on fare revenue rather than expanded local revenue tools shifts more of the financing burden onto riders if higher orders of government do not renew or expand support, since fare revenue funds roughly a third of operating costs and ridership has not recovered to 2019 levels (FAO Ontario, 2024).

#### Chow's mechanism
Chow's approach combines an expanded Municipal Land Transfer Tax on luxury homes (passed at council December 2025, taking effect April 2026), a fare freeze plus monthly fare-capping at 47 paid trips through PRESTO, continued use of the City Building Fund levy for transit and housing capital, and active lobbying for federal and provincial operating support including a renewed New Deal (CBC, December 2025; Toronto Life, December 2025; City of Toronto 2026 Budget, February 2026). Incidence work on land transfer taxes finds the statutory burden falls on home buyers, with empirical evidence that a non-trivial share is capitalized into prices and reduces transaction volumes (Dachis, Duranton and Turner, 2012); fare-cap and fare-freeze incidence studies find the largest gains accrue to riders making 30 plus trips a month, who are disproportionately lower-income commuters (TransitCenter, 2021).

### POSITIONS

#### Brad Bradford
Bradford has voted against Mayor Chow's 2024 (9.5 percent), 2025 (6.9 percent), and 2026 (2.2 percent) property tax and City Building Fund packages, arguing that residents are paying more without receiving improved service, and has criticized the use of reserve funds to balance the operating budget (CBC News, February 2024; CBC News, February 2025; CBC News, December 2025). Bradford's stated transit priorities, as a TTC board member and declared 2026 mayoral candidate, are fare-evasion enforcement, building the Eglinton East LRT, and reliance on federal and provincial funding rather than new local revenue tools (TorontoToday, April 2026; NOW Toronto, March 2026).

#### Olivia Chow
Chow's 2026 budget held the property-tax-plus-City-Building-Fund increase to 2.2 percent, delivered a $1.48 billion TTC subsidy, froze fares for a third year, and introduced PRESTO monthly fare capping at 47 paid trips effective September 1, 2026, with a planned reduction to 40 trips in 2027 (City of Toronto, February 2026; CP24, December 2025). Chow's executive committee has previously advanced a commercial parking levy, an expanded MLTT on homes over $3 million (passed December 2025, effective April 2026), and federal-provincial cost-sharing on the Waterfront East LRT and other capital projects (Toronto Life, December 2025; CBC News, March 2026).

### STATUS QUO

The TTC's 2026 operating budget is approximately $3.03 billion, up roughly $210 million from 2025, with a city subsidy of $1.48 billion (about 49 percent of operating expenses) and a $35 million draw on TTC reserves to hold fares constant (CBC News, December 2025). In 2022, the TTC funded 35.9 percent of operating expenses from own-source revenue and 64.1 percent from government subsidies; the operating subsidy per ride rose from $1.18 in 2019 to $2.62 in 2024 as ridership recovery lagged costs (FAO Ontario, 2024; Globe and Mail, 2024). 2026 ridership is projected at 426 million trips, roughly 81 percent of the 525 million carried in 2019 (TorontoToday, December 2025). The Ontario-Toronto "New Deal" of November 2023 included $330 million per year in operating support tied to provincial transit lines (Eglinton Crosstown, Finch West) over three years and expires in 2027; TTC documents project a $379 million operating shortfall in 2027 and $145 million in 2028 if it is not renewed (Government of Ontario, 2023; TorontoToday, December 2025).

#### Existing policy stack
- City of Toronto 2026 Budget: 2.2 percent combined residential property-tax plus City Building Fund increase (1.5 percent of which is the dedicated transit-and-housing City Building Fund), $1.48 billion TTC subsidy, $35 million reserve draw (City of Toronto, February 2026).
- TTC fare freeze for 2026 plus monthly fare cap at 47 PRESTO trips effective September 1, 2026 (CP24, December 2025).
- Ontario gas tax to municipal transit: roughly $178 million to Toronto in FY 2024-25, split roughly equally between TTC operating and capital (Steve Munro on TTC subsidy streams, March 2026, derived from City of Toronto budget data).
- Ontario-Toronto "New Deal" (signed November 2023): $1.2 billion in transit-related support, $330 million per year in dedicated operating funding for provincial lines for three years, expires 2027 (Government of Ontario, 2023; CBC News, 2023).
- Federal Canada Public Transit Fund (announced July 2024): $30 billion over ten years; Toronto's baseline allocation announced at $1.2 billion over ten years, primarily capital (Prime Minister of Canada, 2024; Department of Finance Canada, 2024).
- Municipal Land Transfer Tax: graduated luxury-tier rates passed December 2025 effective April 2026, projected to add $13.8 million in 2026 revenue, with council motion to allocate increase to transit and housing (CBC News, December 2025; Toronto Life, December 2025).
- Commercial Parking Levy: under study, deferred in early 2025; staff and external (KPMG, Access Planning) estimates of $100 to $150 million annual gross revenue (City of Toronto CFO Update, January 2025).

### COMPARABLE JURISDICTIONS

#### Stockholm (2006 to present)
Stockholm introduced a cordon-based congestion charge in January 2006 as a trial, then permanently from August 2007, with revenues directed to transport investment. Net annual revenues reached approximately 1.3 billion SEK by the late 2010s and operating costs fell to roughly 7 percent of revenue; the system reduced cordon traffic by approximately 20 to 22 percent and was found socially beneficial in ex-post cost-benefit analysis with infrastructure investment recovered in the order of three to four years (Eliasson, 2009, T3; Börjesson and Kristoffersson, 2018, T3). Caveats: Stockholm's transit network is denser per capita than Toronto's, the legal regime is national rather than municipal, and the 2006 baseline economy and fuel-price environment differ from Toronto in 2026.

#### London (2003 to present)
London introduced a daytime central-zone congestion charge in February 2003, with statutory ring-fencing of net revenue to transport investment. Leape (2006, T3) reports a 21 percent reduction in chargeable traffic and significant mode shift; Transport for London has reported gross revenue of approximately 2.6 billion pounds over the first decade with about 1.2 billion pounds reinvested in public transport, road, and active-transport improvements (TfL Annual Reports, T1). Caveats: net revenue has flattened since 2013 as private-hire vehicle volumes grew; the scheme operates under UK national legislation and is administered by a regional transport authority with revenue-recycling rules that Toronto does not currently have.

#### New York (2025 to present)
The MTA's Central Business District tolling program (congestion pricing) launched January 5, 2025, after years of legal and political delay, with revenue legally dedicated to backing $15 billion in MTA capital borrowing. In its first year, MTA and the New York Governor's office reported approximately $500 million in net revenue, an 11 percent reduction in vehicles entering the zone, and 27 million fewer vehicle entries year over year (Office of Governor Hochul, December 2025, T1; MTA, 2025, T1). On the operating side, the Payroll Mobility Tax was increased in 2023 and now generates roughly $3.1 billion annually, which is the MTA's single largest tax source of operating revenue at about 40 percent of own-source operating revenue (Office of the New York State Comptroller, May 2025, T1). Caveats: New York's authority to levy a regional payroll tax and a CBD toll comes from state legislation; Toronto would require equivalent provincial enabling action.

#### Metro Vancouver / TransLink (2010s to present)
TransLink funds operations from a regional motor-fuel tax (set at 18.5 cents per litre), parking sales tax, fare revenue, and a regional property-tax share. Approximately 25 percent of TransLink's operating revenue, on the order of $400 million annually, has historically come from the fuel tax, but staff and independent analysis have flagged this revenue source as declining due to fuel-economy improvements and electrification (TransLink, 2018, T1). The 2018 Mobility Pricing Independent Commission estimated that a regional distance-based road charge could raise $1 billion to $1.6 billion annually if a fuel-tax replacement design were chosen (TransLink Mobility Pricing Independent Commission, 2018, T1). Caveats: TransLink is a regional authority with provincial legislative backing for these tools; Toronto has narrower revenue authority under the City of Toronto Act, 2006.

### PROJECTIONS
Literature does not support a confident singular projection on this question. The comparable-jurisdiction outcomes above are the closest defensible numerical anchors. Plural ranges that are defensible from the cited sources:
- TTC operating gap absent New Deal renewal: $379 million in 2027 and $145 million in 2028 per TTC budget documents (TorontoToday, December 2025; TTC 2026 Operating Budget Notes, City of Toronto, 2026).
- Toronto commercial parking levy gross revenue range: $100 million to $150 million annually per City of Toronto CFO and external consultants (City of Toronto, January 2025).
- Toronto MLTT luxury-tier expansion: roughly $13.8 million incremental revenue in 2026 against a projected luxury-tier total of approximately $152 million (Toronto Life, December 2025; City of Toronto, December 2025).
- Stockholm-type cordon charge in a comparable jurisdiction: net revenue on the order of 7 to 9 percent annual transit ridership lift during initial trial periods, with capital costs recovered in roughly three to four years in the Stockholm case (Eliasson, 2009; Börjesson and Kristoffersson, 2018). Direct application to Toronto requires assumptions about provincial enabling legislation, charging boundary, and price level that the literature does not settle.

### TIME HORIZON
Operating-funding effects materialize on a one to three year horizon: the New Deal expiry in 2027 forces a financing decision in the 2027 and 2028 budgets, while revenue tools that require provincial enabling (cordon pricing, payroll-style mobility taxes) typically take three to seven years from authorization to first revenue based on the New York and London experiences (Leape, 2006; MTA, 2025).
