# Mayoral Record . Policy Scenario Card . Card 4 of 5

## TOPIC

How Toronto allocates effort between sworn policing, non-police crisis response, harm reduction, and community-investment programs, and what the comparable-jurisdiction evidence says about the safety outcomes of each mix.

## SLUG

public-safety-approach

## PULL QUOTE

Bradford emphasizes more sworn officers, visible enforcement, and faster response. Chow emphasizes embedding non-police crisis teams alongside police, expanding community investment, and continuing harm-reduction support. The peer-reviewed and program-evaluation literature finds modest crime-reduction effects from added police, larger arrest-diversion and access-to-care effects from civilian crisis response, and population-level overdose-mortality reductions in the immediate vicinity of supervised consumption sites. Most effects are conditional on local context, scale, and time period.

## WHO BENEFITS

### Bradford's mechanism

Bradford's emphasis on hiring more officers, deploying police at every TTC subway station, and prioritizing visible enforcement is directed at riders, residents in high-call-volume neighbourhoods, and small-business operators concerned about disorder. Quasi-experimental research on U.S. police-hiring grants (Mello, 2019) and measurement-error-corrected city panels (Chalfin and McCrary, 2018) finds that marginal additions of police are associated with reductions in serious crime in the range of roughly 0.3 to 0.4 percent for each 1 percent increase in officers, but the same literature documents that hiring expansions in the United States have raised low-level arrests for minor offences. Effects materialize in years 1 to 3 and depend on deployment strategy, not headcount alone (College of Policing, 2021).

### Chow's mechanism

Chow's emphasis on the citywide Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS), the Mobile Crisis Intervention Team (MCIT) co-response model, expanded transit-station staffing, and SafeTO youth-prevention spending is directed at people in mental-health crisis, transit riders, and youth in priority neighbourhoods, with reductions in police use of force and emergency-department wait time as the most-evidenced near-term outcomes (Lamanna et al., MCIT outcome evaluation, 2018). The CAHOOTS evaluation (Davis et al., NBER, 2025) and Toronto's own one-year TCCS evaluation (City of Toronto, 2023) show large reductions in arrests for crisis-coded calls and high client satisfaction. The literature is thinner on whether civilian crisis response by itself reduces overall violent crime.

## POSITIONS

### Brad Bradford

Bradford has called for the deployment of police officers in every TTC subway station, supports the multi-year police hiring plan, and has proposed adding 50 sworn officers and 40 special constables to TTC "hot spots," along with platform-edge doors and updated cleanliness standards (CBC News, 2023; TorontoToday, 2026). He has publicly supported Toronto Police Service budget increases each year of his council tenure and has framed the 2026 race around "core services" and a service review to identify cuts elsewhere (Toronto Today, 2026; Beach Metro Community News, 2025).

### Olivia Chow

Chow launched the Toronto Community Crisis Service citywide on September 26, 2024, hiring more than 100 additional crisis workers and embedding TCCS teams 24/7 on Line 1 between Spadina and Bloor-Yonge stations as of November 15, 2025 (City of Toronto, 2024; TTC News, 2025). She also supported the Toronto Police Service's $46.2 million budget increase for 2025 funding 109 net new officers, and added $5 million to SafeTO for summer youth programs (CBC News, 2024; City of Toronto, 2025).

## STATUS QUO

Toronto's 2024 Crime Severity Index was 68.7, below the national figure of 77.89, with the Violent CSI at 99.8 (up roughly 3 percent year over year) and the Non-violent CSI at 56.8 (Statistics Canada, Police-reported Crime Severity Index by CMA, 2024). Homicides totalled 84 in 2024 (up from 73 in 2023), and shooting events rose to 461 with 43 firearm-related deaths, but as of December 18, 2025 the city had recorded 39 homicides, a 55 percent decrease from the same point in 2024, on pace for the lowest annual total in roughly two decades (Toronto Police Service, 2024 Chief's Annual Report; Global News, 2025). Auto thefts fell from 12,501 in 2023 to 9,570 in 2024 and dropped a further 25 to 34 percent through 2025 (Toronto Today, 2025; Équité Association, 2025). The 2025 Toronto Police Service operating budget was approved at roughly $1.22 billion, and the 2026 budget rose to a net $1.43 billion, including 143 net new officers in 2026 in addition to the officers hired in 2025 (Toronto Police Service Board, 2024; CBC News, 2026).

### Existing policy stack

- Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS), launched citywide September 26, 2024, with 12 mobile teams and approximately 200 staff. In its first year of operation it received 6,827 calls for service and resolved 78 percent of 911-transferred calls without police involvement (City of Toronto news release, September 2024; CBC News, 2024; T1).
- Mobile Crisis Intervention Team (MCIT), a co-responder model pairing a police officer with a mental-health nurse, operating since 2002. The Centre for Research on Inner City Health 2014 evaluation reported a 56-minute mean ED wait time for MCIT cases versus 85 minutes for Primary Response Unit calls, and injuries in 2 percent of MCIT interactions (Lamanna et al., St. Michael's Hospital MCIT Outcome Evaluation, 2014; T2).
- 9-1-1 Crisis Call Diversion (CCD) pilot, partnered with Gerstein Crisis Centre, with reported 65 percent diversion rate (Toronto Police Service, news release, 2023; T1).
- SafeTO 10-year community safety and well-being plan, with a budget of more than $40 million plus a $5 million 2025 top-up for summer youth programs (City of Toronto, 2025; T1).
- Toronto Police Service multi-year hiring plan adopted by the Toronto Police Service Board in November 2024, projected to add 502 net new officers through 2029 (Toronto Police Service Board, 2024; T1).
- 2025 Auditor General report and prior 2022 Auditor General review of TPS calls for service, which found 47 percent of low-priority calls were dispatched to front-line officers in 2019 and recommended alternative-response routing (Office of the Auditor General of Toronto, 2022; T1).
- Five Toronto supervised consumption sites were closed by provincial order on March 31, 2025 under Ontario's Safer Streets, Stronger Communities Act, with provincial transition to abstinence-based HART hubs (CBC News, 2024 and 2025; T2).

## COMPARABLE JURISDICTIONS

### Eugene, Oregon (CAHOOTS), 1989 to 2023

Eugene's CAHOOTS program dispatches a non-uniformed pair (a crisis worker and a medic) to mental-health, homelessness, and addiction-related 911 calls in place of, or alongside, police. In 2021 CAHOOTS responded to 16,479 of 109,855 public-initiated calls to Eugene Police Department, roughly 15 percent of EPD call volume, on a 2022 contract value of approximately $820,586 against an EPD budget of about $68 million (White Bird Clinic; National League of Cities; T2). Davis, Norris, Schmitt, Shem-Tov and Strickland (NBER WP 33761, 2025) exploited geographic and temporal expansions of CAHOOTS and found that the probability a 911 call would result in an arrest fell by 76 percent in newly served areas, and subsequent 911 call volume in those areas declined (T3). Caveats: CAHOOTS operated in a city about one twentieth Toronto's population with different drug-poisoning, gun-violence, and policing baselines, and the contract relationship ended in spring 2023 in Eugene proper.

### Vancouver Downtown Eastside (Insite and supervised consumption), 2003 to 2024

Vancouver opened Insite in September 2003 as North America's first sanctioned supervised consumption site. Marshall et al. (Lancet, 2011, doi 10.1016/S0140-6736(10)62353-7) found a 35 percent reduction in overdose mortality within 500 metres of Insite in the post-opening period, compared with a 9 percent decrease in the rest of Vancouver (T3). Potier et al. (Drug and Alcohol Dependence, 2014, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2014.10.012) systematically reviewed 75 studies and concluded that supervised injection services reduced overdose frequency and increased access to primary care without increasing drug use, trafficking, or surrounding crime (T3). A 2022 PLOS One controlled interrupted time-series of British Columbia health areas opening sites between 2015 and 2017 found no significant province-level reduction in opioid mortality (Kennedy et al., 2022; T3). British Columbia recorded 2,253 unregulated drug toxicity deaths in 2024, a 13 percent decrease from 2023, with about 22 percent of deaths in Vancouver (BC Coroners Service, 2025; T1). Caveats: only about 5 percent of injections in DTES historically occur inside a site, and most overdose fatalities happen in private residences during solitary use.

### Glasgow Violence Reduction Unit (Scotland), 2005 to 2020

Strathclyde Police launched the Scottish Violence Reduction Unit in 2005, treating violence as a preventable disease through education, focused-deterrence outreach to gang-involved youth, and partnerships with health services. Williams, Currie, Linden, and Donnelly (Aggression and Violent Behavior, 2014, doi 10.1016/j.avb.2014.09.011) reported a 52 percent reduction in offending among intervention participants over two years versus 29 percent in matched comparison youth, and an 84 percent reduction in weapon-carrying versus 40 percent (T3). Police-recorded non-sexual violent crime in Scotland fell 48 percent from 2006-07 onward, with a 38 percent fall in homicide. Fraser and Gillon (Theoretical Criminology, 2024, doi 10.1177/13624806231208432) document the public-narrative and political conditions behind the change but caution explicitly that causal attribution to the SVRU alone is contested (T3). Caveats: Scotland operates under different drug, alcohol, firearm, and youth-justice regimes than Ontario, and several confounders (recession, demographic change, alcohol-pricing policy) overlap the time window.

### Camden, New Jersey (police restructuring), 2013 to 2025

Camden disbanded its municipal police department in 2013 and rebuilt it as the Camden County Police Department, retaining many officers after re-application and shifting deployment toward foot-and-bike patrol and community engagement. Homicides fell from 67 in 2012 to 23 in 2017 and to 12 in 2025, an 82 percent decline since 2012, with violent crime down by approximately 53 percent over the same period (Camden County, 2025; The Inquirer, 2026; WHYY, 2024; T2). Caveats: Camden's reform required a complete labour-relations reset that is not legally available in Toronto, summons for low-level offences increased in early years (ACLU of New Jersey, 2015), and Camden's population is roughly 70,000, two orders of magnitude smaller than Toronto.

### Calgary Police and Crisis Team (PACT), 2008 to 2025

Calgary's PACT pairs an Alberta Health Services mental-health professional with a Calgary Police Service officer to respond to mental-health, addictions, and psychosocial crises. PACT expanded from 6 to 12 teams operating 22 hours per day, and Calgary Police Service committed approximately $8 million per year since 2021 to the Community Safety Investment Framework that supports PACT and partner programs (Calgary Police Service, 2024; Alberta Health Services, 2024; T1, T2). Independent peer-reviewed outcome data on PACT's effect on use of force, hospital diversion, or crime is more limited than for CAHOOTS or TCCS. Caveats: PACT remains a co-responder model, not a fully civilian model.

## PROJECTIONS

The peer-reviewed and program-evaluation literature supports the following plural projections, applied with explicit caveats:

1. Adding 100 to 150 net officers per year (the current Toronto trajectory) is consistent with marginal serious-crime reductions in the order of small single-digit percentages over years 1 to 3, with the size of the effect strongly dependent on deployment strategy and unevenly distributed across crime types. Mello (2019) and Chalfin and McCrary (2018) bracket the best available elasticities. Several reviews (Lee, Eck, Corsaro, 2016) find smaller or null effects, so a confident point estimate is not warranted.

2. Maintaining and scaling TCCS to a footprint comparable to CAHOOTS at maturity (15 percent of public-initiated calls) is consistent with sustained 70 to 80 percent diversion of crisis-coded 911 calls without police, and with reductions in arrest probability for those calls in the range observed by Davis et al. (2025). The literature does not yet support a singular projection of TCCS effect on overall violent-crime rates.

3. The closure of five Toronto supervised consumption sites in March 2025, in the context of the Marshall (2011) and Potier (2014) literature, is consistent with localized increases in opioid-related EMS calls and ED visits in the immediate vicinity of the closed sites. Toronto Paramedic Services data cited by former Toronto mayors recorded an 82 percent year-over-year increase in suspected opioid-overdose calls between April 2025 and January 2026 (Toronto Today, 2026; T2). Population-level mortality effects across the city are harder to attribute given the BC controlled time-series finding (Kennedy et al., 2022).

Beyond these three, the literature does not support a confident singular projection on whether a Bradford-style mix or a Chow-style mix produces lower aggregate violent-crime rates in Toronto over four years. The comparable-jurisdiction outcomes above are the closest defensible numerical anchors.

## TIME HORIZON

Police-hiring effects on serious-crime rates appear in years 1 to 3 (Mello, 2019). Civilian crisis-response effects on arrest probability and ED throughput appear within months of program activation in served geographies (Davis et al., 2025; Toronto Community Crisis Service One-Year Outcome Evaluation Report, 2023). Public-health violence-reduction effects on the Glasgow scale require 5 to 15 years to accumulate and remain difficult to attribute to a single program (Fraser and Gillon, 2024).
