# Mayoral Record . Policy Scenario . Card 5 of 5

### TOPIC
City spending on parks, ravines, urban forest, and climate-resilience infrastructure over the next council term.

### SLUG
climate-parks-investment

### PULL QUOTE
Toronto's documented basement-flood and stormwater backlog totals roughly 1,200 recommended projects valued near $18 billion, while the July 15 to 16, 2024 storm produced an estimated $940 million in insured damages across Toronto and southern Ontario. The two leading candidates are running on opposing answers to that gap: accelerated public investment in ravines, urban forest, and stormwater works versus restraint on the property-tax line that pays for it.

### WHO BENEFITS

#### Bradford's mechanism
Bradford has framed his platform around "the three Cs" (crime, congestion, cost of living) and has voted against recent property-tax increases used to fund expanded parks and climate operating spending (NOW Toronto, October 2, 2025; CBC, February 2025). Restraining the tax line tends to lower the carrying cost of property in the short run, with incidence research finding that capping property-tax growth disproportionately benefits owners of higher-value properties because the dollar value of foregone tax growth scales with assessment (Crompton, 2001, T3). Where reduced parks and stormwater investment leaves the basement-flooding and ravine state-of-good-repair backlog in place, incidence of avoided damages falls on properties without exposure, while properties in flood-prone catchments and lower-canopy neighbourhoods continue to absorb risk (Toronto Auditor General, 2025, T1; David Suzuki Foundation, 2022, T2). Timeframe: tax-relief incidence is immediate. Avoided-damage incidence runs over 5 to 30 years and depends on storm recurrence.

#### Chow's mechanism
Chow's 2026 budget continues the Ravine Strategy, urban-forest expansion (target of 121,700 trees planted in 2025), and basement-flooding subsidy enhancements, funded in part through a 2.2 percent property-tax increase (City of Toronto, February 10, 2026, T1; City of Toronto 2025 PFR Budget Notes, T1). Hedonic-pricing research finds that proximity to parks and tree canopy is capitalized into property values, with Crompton's review of 30 studies finding a 20 percent uplift on properties abutting passive parkland as a defensible central estimate (Crompton, 2001, T3) and Bockarjova et al.'s meta-analysis of 37 hedonic studies finding positive but heterogeneous capitalization across urban green types (Bockarjova et al., 2020, T3). Same-framework caveat: those uplifts also raise rents and assessments, with documented green-gentrification risks where investment is concentrated in low-canopy neighbourhoods without housing-affordability offsets (Bockarjova and Botzen, 2020, T3). Timeframe: capital projects deliver flood-mitigation benefits over years 3 to 10. Canopy benefits compound over decades.

### POSITIONS

#### Brad Bradford
Bradford filed mayoral nomination on May 1, 2026, and has stated his campaign priorities are "crime, congestion and the cost of living," with a detailed platform deferred to the 2026 campaign cycle (NOW Toronto, October 2, 2025, T2; CP24, May 1, 2026, T2). On the most recent budget vote, Bradford was among councillors voting against the 6.9 percent property-tax increase that funded expanded parks, climate, and forestry operating spending (CBC News, February 2025, T2).

#### Olivia Chow
Chow's 2026 budget continues the Ravine Strategy, the InTO the Ravines program with Park People, basement-flooding subsidy enhancements, and the Net Zero Strategy Action Plan 2026 to 2030 adopted by Council on December 17, 2025 (City of Toronto, February 10, 2026, T1; City of Toronto Ravine Strategy 2026 Implementation Update, February 2026, T1; City of Toronto TransformTO page, T1).

### STATUS QUO
Toronto Water's 10-year capital plan (2024 to 2033) allocated approximately $2.1 billion for the Basement Flooding Protection Program and $2.4 billion for stormwater management including the Don River and Central Waterfront project, against a recommended-project pipeline valued near $18 billion (City of Toronto Schedule B 2024 to 2028 Project List, T1; Toronto Water capital plan reporting, T1). The Parks, Forestry and Recreation 10-year capital plan for 2025 to 2034 totals $4.3 billion, with an accumulated state-of-good-repair backlog projected to exceed $1.1 billion by 2025 (City of Toronto 2025 PFR Budget Notes, T1). The July 15 to 16, 2024 storm produced approximately $940 million in insured damages in Toronto and southern Ontario per Catastrophe Indices and Quantification (Insurance Bureau of Canada, August 2024, T2). City-wide emissions in 2021 (most recent inventory) were 41 percent below 1990 levels against a 45 percent by 2025 target (City of Toronto TransformTO Net Zero Progress Report, 2024, T1).

#### Existing policy stack
- Ravine Strategy and Implementation Plan: $39.8 million from City and $18.2 million from Government of Canada committed since 2020, with the 2022 reporting year showing $118.1 million in ravine-system investment across operating and capital lines (City of Toronto Ravine Strategy 2022 Implementation Update, T1; Water Canada coverage, T2).
- Basement Flooding Protection Program: 67 study areas complete, approximately 1,200 recommended projects valued near $18 billion in construction costs (City of Toronto Reducing Stormwater Runoff staff report, 2025, T1).
- Don River and Central Waterfront Project: Coxwell Bypass Tunnel under construction with completion targeted for 2026, Inner Harbour West and Taylor-Massey Creek Tunnels not expected before 2036 (City of Toronto, T1).
- Port Lands Flood Protection: approximately $1.4 billion across three orders of government, with Don River mouth completed November 2024 (Waterfront Toronto, T1).
- Urban-forestry tree-planting target: 121,700 trees in 2025 with goal of 40 percent canopy cover by 2050 (City of Toronto 2025 PFR Budget Notes, T1).
- TransformTO Net Zero Strategy Action Plan 2026 to 2030 adopted December 17, 2025 (City of Toronto, T1).
- Carbon Budget tied to financial budget: 2025 Carbon Budget projected emissions reductions of 65,858 tCO2e in 2025 rising to 244,615 tCO2e per year at full implementation across 31 projects (City of Toronto Carbon Budget, January 15, 2025, T1).

### COMPARABLE JURISDICTIONS

**Copenhagen, cloudburst plan (post-2011 to present).**
After a 2 July 2011 cloudburst caused over $1 billion in damage, Copenhagen adopted a Cloudburst Management Plan with more than 300 surface and blue-green interventions across seven catchments, costing roughly DKK 13 billion versus a DKK 20 billion traditional sewer alternative. Climate-ADAPT and HOFOR analyses report the alternative blue-green plan delivered higher net socio-economic benefits and avoided damages estimated at $60 to 90 million per year through to 2110 absent action (Climate-ADAPT case study, T2; State of Green, T2). Caveat: legal and utility-finance regime differs from Toronto. The Greater Copenhagen Utility (HOFOR) co-finances works through wastewater tariffs in ways Toronto Water does not currently mirror.

**Calgary, post-2013 flood mitigation (2013 to present).**
Following the June 2013 floods (CA$5 billion total damage, CA$1.7 billion insured per public reporting), Alberta and Calgary invested over $1.3 billion in flood-mitigation and resilience projects. The City reports mitigation work has reduced flood risk by approximately 70 percent and reduced annual average flood damages by approximately $118 million per year, with a stated benefit-cost ratio near 10 to 1 in avoided damages (City of Calgary, T1; Government of Alberta, T1). Caveat: river-flooding hydrology (Bow and Elbow Rivers) differs from Toronto's combined-sewer urban flooding pattern. Some headline benefit-cost figures come from the City and Province themselves, not independent peer review.

**New York Hudson River Park (waterfront completion phases, 2000s to present).**
The 2008 Regional Plan Association and Friends of Hudson River Park study found that completion of the Greenwich Village section raised real-estate prices in the adjacent two blocks by approximately 20 percent, with park-attributable property value across the study area estimated at roughly $200 million; from 2000 to 2015 property-tax revenue in the park-adjacent neighbourhood grew approximately 230 percent versus approximately 180 percent in the rest of Manhattan (Regional Plan Association, 2008, T2; Realizing the Benefits of Hudson River Park, RPA, 2018, T2). Caveat: Manhattan waterfront pricing dynamics are not transferable to most Toronto ravine edges. Property-uplift findings also imply displacement and rent-pressure risks not addressed in the original studies.

**Singapore Park Connector Network (1991 to present).**
The Park Connector Network now exceeds 90 connectors linking parks and green spaces, supporting walking, jogging, and cycling at metropolitan scale (National Parks Board, T1; Wikipedia summary citing NParks, T2). Peer-reviewed research finds park access associated with higher physical activity but with effect sizes that vary by demographic and built-environment context (Petrunoff et al., PMC8254359, T3). Caveat: Singapore is a city-state with unitary planning authority and tropical climate. Direct transfer to Toronto's bi-level governance and seasonal climate is limited.

**Stockholm congestion charge and environmental zone (2007 to present).**
The Stockholm congestion tax reduced central-city traffic volumes by roughly 20 percent, with PM10 and NO2 declining 10 to 20 percent relative to pre-charge levels and reduced severe asthma incidence in children (Simeonova et al., NBER Working Paper 24410, 2018, T2; Burman and Johansson, peer-reviewed work on congestion-tax air quality, T3). Caveat: the strongest health and emissions effects come from congestion pricing, not the low-emission zone alone. Toronto has no congestion-pricing regime, so this comparable speaks to a class of policy not currently on either candidate's table.

### PROJECTIONS
Literature does not support a confident singular projection on this question. The comparable-jurisdiction outcomes above are the closest defensible numerical anchors. Plural plausible ranges supported by the cited literature:

- Park-proximity property-value capitalization: across Crompton's 2001 review, a 20 percent uplift on properties directly abutting passive parkland is a defensible central estimate, with effects falling sharply with distance and varying by park type. Bockarjova and Botzen's 2020 meta-analysis finds positive but heterogeneous effects across green-intervention types, and flags green-gentrification risk as a documented co-effect.
- Flood-mitigation benefit-cost ratios: Copenhagen and Calgary self-report high benefit-cost ratios (Copenhagen finding the blue-green alternative cheaper than sewer-only by roughly DKK 7 billion in capital cost, Calgary reporting near 10 to 1). Independent peer-reviewed studies of green stormwater infrastructure report a wider range of benefit-cost ratios depending on scale, type, and co-benefits monetized (Heidari, Wiley Interdisciplinary Reviews Water, 2023, T3).

### TIME HORIZON
Tax-line and operating-spending effects materialize in year 1. Capital flood-mitigation works (tunnels, ravine restoration, basement-flooding studies-to-construction) deliver protection over years 3 to 15, with the Don River and Central Waterfront tunnel system not fully complete until 2036 per current City reporting. Urban-canopy and ravine ecological-health benefits compound over 20 to 50 years.
